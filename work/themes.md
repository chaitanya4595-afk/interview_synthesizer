# Themes

## Theme 1: Doubling chain-wide in two years is widely seen as a stretch, and stakeholders favor staging growth by category and store cluster instead of one chain-wide number.
Support: 5 interviews [INT01, INT03, INT04, INT05, INT08] | Against: 1 [INT02]
Key quotes:
* "I'll be honest, I think that's a stretch in two years." [INT08]
* "I think we need more of it, but doubling in two years feels like a headquarters number, not a store number." [INT03]
* "To double in two, we'd need roughly 400 new items, a full packaging redesign, significant supplier investment, and store resets across every store." [INT08]
Why it matters for the case question: It suggests the answer to doubling in two years is no as a single chain-wide target, but yes to a staged path led by ready categories and value-market stores [INT02 calls doubling "overdue", yet also asks for a category-by-category rollout with ready stores first].

## Theme 2: Store brand is trusted on basics, but cost-cut specs, weak flavor-driven items such as coffee, and dated packaging give it a "cheap" image that blocks growth.
Support: 8 interviews [INT01, INT02, INT03, INT04, INT05, INT06, INT07, INT08] | Against: 0
Key quotes:
* "You can't double share with a brand people are embarrassed to put in their cart." [INT08]
* "Honestly, quality is usually a spec problem, not a manufacturing problem." [INT04]
* "You lose somebody on one product and they don't try the next one." [INT01]
Why it matters for the case question: Doubling would require relaunching the brand, with better specs and packaging, before adding more items; otherwise more shelf space goes to products shoppers don't trust.

## Theme 3: Suppliers can support growth in dry grocery, but dairy, frozen and fresh are near capacity, and suppliers will invest only against firm volume commitments and better terms.
Support: 7 interviews [INT01, INT02, INT03, INT04, INT05, INT06, INT08] | Against: 0
Key quotes:
* "Our lines are running at about 70% utilization right now, so there's room." [INT04]
* "For dairy and frozen, doubling in two years is not realistic unless they bring in more suppliers, and bringing in new suppliers has its own risks." [INT05]
* "So before we add people or equipment, we'd want a multi-year agreement with minimum volumes." [INT04]
Why it matters for the case question: Supply capacity decides which categories can realistically grow. It also means Northfield must commit volume and improve payment terms, and fix today's store-brand out-of-stocks.

## Theme 4: The margin gain is real, but it holds only if shoppers see a clear price gap, and it shrinks once spec, packaging and trade-fund costs are counted.
Support: 7 interviews [INT02, INT03, INT04, INT05, INT06, INT07, INT08] | Against: 0
Key quotes:
* "Our private label items carry a gross margin somewhere around 32 to 35%, compared to about 25% on the comparable national brands." [INT08]
* "The price gap has to be real, like 20 to 30% under the brand, not five cents." [INT02]
* "I'd estimate we could lose a meaningful part of trade funds in the categories we shift most aggressively." [INT08]
Why it matters for the case question: The business case has to be modeled net of lost trade funds and higher spec costs, and it has to give shoppers a visible price cut, not only capture margin.

## Theme 5: Discounters make a private label price story necessary, but pulling the brands shoppers are loyal to risks sending them to competitors.
Support: 6 interviews [INT01, INT02, INT03, INT06, INT07, INT08] | Against: 0
Key quotes:
* "We're kind of stuck in the middle if we don't move." [INT02]
* "If I take their ketchup off the shelf, I'm giving them a reason to leave too." [INT03]
* "Once I'm going somewhere else for a few things, I might start going there for more things." [INT06]
Why it matters for the case question: Growth should add strong store-brand options alongside the brands shoppers are loyal to, not remove those brands wholesale.

## Theme 6: Price-pressed shoppers are already trading into store brand and would try more with samples or coupons, but affluent stores and snack categories have not responded.
Support: 3 interviews [INT02, INT06, INT07] | Against: 2 [INT01, INT08]
Key quotes:
* "The last couple of years, with prices going up on everything, I've watched people trade down in real time." [INT02]
* "If they gave out samples or had a money-back guarantee, I'd be more willing." [INT06]
* "People here don't really shop us on price, honestly." [INT01]
Why it matters for the case question: Demand is uneven, so the rollout should start in value-focused stores and staple categories and use trial tools, not assume every store and aisle will follow.

## Where interviewees disagree
* Pace of doubling: [INT02] calls it "overdue", while [INT08] calls it "a stretch in two years" and [INT03] calls it "a headquarters number, not a store number".
* Supplier capacity: [INT04] says "there's room" at about 70% utilization, while [INT05] runs "close to 90%" and can add only "maybe 20% more volume". This is a split by category (dry grocery vs dairy and frozen), not a conflict of facts.
* Price sensitivity of shoppers: [INT02] has "watched people trade down in real time", while [INT01] says "People here don't really shop us on price, honestly."
* What sends shoppers to competitors: [INT02] says missing store-brand items send shoppers "to the discounter up the street", while [INT01] and [INT06] say missing brands send them to "the other grocer down the road" or elsewhere.
* Size of the margin gain: [INT08] cites 32 to 35% vs about 25% and [INT02] "roughly 8 points more", while [INT05] says in dairy "it's smaller in dairy than people think" and [INT04] warns that paying for a better spec "might mean the margin is a few points lower than they hoped".
* Dairy quality: [INT02] lists dairy among staples that "are as good as the brand", while [INT05] and [INT01] describe a store-brand yogurt problem that drove complaints and stopped trial.
