Interview ID: INT04
Role: Private label supplier (dry grocery co-packer)
SYNTHETIC: fictional interview for demonstration

Interviewer: Thanks for speaking with us. Can you describe your business and your relationship with Northfield?

Interviewee: We're a mid-sized co-packer. Dry grocery mostly: pasta, rice, canned vegetables, some sauces and baking mixes. We make private label for a number of retailers, and Northfield is one of our larger regional customers. We've worked with them for a long time.

Interviewer: If Northfield decided to double its private label share over the next two years, could you support that?

Interviewee: In our categories, mostly yes, with conditions. Our lines are running at about 70% utilization right now, so there's room. We could take on a meaningful chunk of new volume without building anything. Where it gets harder is new items. If they want us to make things we don't make today, that's a different timeline.

Interviewer: What does that timeline look like?

Interviewee: For a new product on an existing line, figure 9 to 12 months from agreed spec to product on the shelf. That includes recipe development, testing, packaging, and the retailer's own approval process, which honestly is often the slowest part. If it needs new equipment, add a lot more. So if Northfield wants to double in two years, the new items basically need to be decided very soon. Otherwise the math doesn't work.

Interviewer: What conditions would you need?

Interviewee: Volume commitments. This is the big one. Retailers love to say "we're going to grow private label" and then the orders don't show up. We've been burned by that. I'm not naming names, but we added a shift for a customer a few years back based on projections and ended up running at half the volume they forecast. So before we add people or equipment, we'd want a multi-year agreement with minimum volumes. That's not unusual. The big national chains do it.

Interviewer: How do you think about quality in this context?

Interviewee: Honestly, quality is usually a spec problem, not a manufacturing problem. We can make a product as good as the national brand. Sometimes we literally make the national brand on the same line. But retailers write specs to hit a cost target, and then they're surprised when the product tastes cheaper. If Northfield wants people to see their brand as equal quality, they have to be willing to pay for the spec. That might mean the margin is a few points lower than they hoped, but the product sells.

Interviewer: What about costs? Are you seeing pressure there?

Interviewee: Packaging is the biggest one. Our packaging costs are up around 18% over the last two years. Cans, film, cartons, all of it. Ingredients have been up and down. Freight has come down a bit. So when retailers ask why the cost of goods went up, it's mostly packaging. And if Northfield wants a packaging refresh, which I'd recommend, their current look is dated, that's a one-time cost too. Plates, artwork, sometimes new materials.

Interviewer: Where do you see the biggest risks in Northfield's plan?

Interviewee: Three things. One, trying to do too many categories at once. Every new category is a new set of suppliers, specs, approvals. Their private label team isn't that big. Two, not committing volume, which I already mentioned. Three, payment terms. Suppliers are going to be nervous about extending more credit. Right now Northfield pays us on 60 days. If they double volume on 60-day terms, that's a lot of working capital sitting on our books. Some smaller suppliers won't be able to handle that.

Interviewer: Do you think doubling is realistic?

Interviewee: In dry grocery, I think it's realistic if they start now and they commit. In other categories I can't speak for. Fresh and frozen are harder. Capacity is tighter there. I'd be careful about assuming all suppliers have spare room like we do. We're a bit unusual. A couple of our competitors are closer to full.

Interviewer: What would make you a better partner in this?

Interviewee: Earlier involvement. Bring us in when they're planning, not after they've picked a target. We can tell them which items are easy wins and which are going to be a headache. Also, share the sales data. We don't see sell-through at the store level, so we're guessing on forecasts. If we could see what's actually selling, we'd plan better and they'd have fewer out-of-stocks.

Interviewer: Anything else you'd like them to know?

Interviewee: Just that we want this to work. More volume is good for us. But we need them to treat it like a partnership, not a purchase order. The retailers who've done this well sit down with their suppliers every quarter and plan it together. The ones who haven't just send a forecast and hope.

Interviewer: Thank you, this is very helpful.

Interviewee: My pleasure.
