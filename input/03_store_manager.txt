Interview ID: INT03
Role: Store manager (rural store, small-town market)
SYNTHETIC: fictional interview for demonstration

Interviewer: Thanks for making time. Can you tell me about your store?

Interviewee: Sure. We're a small-town store, a long drive from the distribution center. We're the main grocery store in town, or we were. A discounter opened about 12 miles away last spring and that changed things. We lost roughly 9% of our transactions in the first six months. Some of that came back, not all of it.

Interviewer: How has private label played into that?

Interviewee: It's complicated. When the discounter opened, the people who left were mostly the price shoppers, and they went there for the private label, basically. Their whole store is store brand. So my first thought was, yeah, we need more private label to compete. But the people who stayed with us, they stayed partly because we carry the brands they know. Farmers, older folks, people who've bought the same ketchup their whole lives. If I take their ketchup off the shelf, I'm giving them a reason to leave too.

Interviewer: So where does that leave you on the idea of doubling?

Interviewee: Somewhere in the middle. I think we need more of it, but doubling in two years feels like a headquarters number, not a store number. My private label is around 19% today. If I got into the mid-twenties, I'd be happy. Doubling would mean pulling a lot of brands, and I don't think my shoppers would follow.

Interviewer: What about the operational side, given your location?

Interviewee: That's actually my biggest concern. We only get a few trucks a week from the DC. When store-brand items are short at the DC, the rural stores get shorted first, or that's how it feels. A city store can get a transfer from another store down the road. I can't. If private label becomes a bigger part of my shelf and the supply chain isn't better than it is today, I'm going to have holes everywhere.

Also, space. My store is old, the back room is small. Private label often comes in bigger case packs because that's how we get the cost down. I don't have room to store that. Last time they changed the case pack on our store-brand paper towels, I had pallets sitting in the aisle for a week.

Interviewer: How do you think your customers see private label quality?

Interviewee: Mixed. On basics, they're fine with it. Frozen vegetables, canned stuff, milk. On anything with a strong taste, not so much. Coffee, peanut butter, barbecue sauce. People around here are particular. I'd also say fresh is a different story. Our store-brand deli meat does really well, better than the brands honestly, and that surprised me. So maybe it's not about more private label everywhere, it's about more of it in the right places.

Interviewer: What about pricing?

Interviewee: The price gap has to be obvious. Right now some of our store-brand items are only 10 or 15 cents cheaper than the brand when the brand is on promotion. Customers see that and think, why bother. The discounter doesn't have that problem because there's no brand next to it to compare.

Interviewer: Do you think the company could source enough product to double?

Interviewee: I don't know, that's above my pay grade. But I'd guess not easily. We already have trouble keeping some items in stock. I'd want someone to ask the suppliers that question before we promise anything to customers.

Interviewer: If you were advising leadership, what would you tell them?

Interviewee: I'd say, don't do it the same way everywhere. Figure out which categories are ready and which aren't. Fix the supply chain first, especially for stores like mine that are far out. And make the price difference big enough to matter. If those things happen, I'm for it. If it's just a percentage target on a slide, I'm worried we'll lose the customers we still have without winning back the ones we lost.

Interviewer: One more question. Is there anything you'd want to see tested first?

Interviewee: Deli and fresh. I'd put store brand in more of fresh before I'd push it in coffee or snacks. We already have proof it works there. And maybe a test in a couple of stores like mine to see what the discounter shoppers do. Do they come back if we have a real store-brand price story? I don't know. I'd like to find out before we bet the chain on it.

Interviewer: Thank you, that's really helpful.

Interviewee: Sure thing.
