Interview ID: INT01
Role: Store manager (suburban store, higher-income area)
SYNTHETIC: fictional interview for demonstration

Interviewer: Thanks for making time. Can you start by telling me a bit about your store and your shoppers?

Interviewee: Sure. We're one of the bigger stores in the chain, out in the suburbs off the highway. A lot of families, a lot of two-income households. People here don't really shop us on price, honestly. They shop us because we're close, the produce is good, and they know where everything is. If they wanted cheapest they'd drive to the discounter, and some of them do, for paper goods and that kind of thing.

Interviewer: How does private label do in your store today?

Interviewee: It's lower than the chain average, I know that. Last time I looked at my own numbers it was around 14% of sales. It moves in the obvious places. Milk, eggs, sugar, flour, foil, trash bags. Nobody cares what brand their sugar is. But you go to coffee, cereal, snacks, anything the kids eat, and it's national brands all the way. I've watched people pick up our store-brand cereal, look at the box, and put it back.

Interviewer: Why do you think they put it back?

Interviewee: Part of it is the box. I'll be honest, some of our packaging looks like it was designed a long time ago, because it probably was. Part of it is habit. And part of it is, frankly, a couple of products that weren't good. We had a store-brand yogurt a while back that customers complained about, the texture was off, and people remember that. You lose somebody on one product and they don't try the next one.

Interviewer: So if the company decided to double private label over the next two years, what would that look like from where you sit?

Interviewee: My first reaction is, doubling from what? In my store that means going from 14 to something like 28. I don't see my shoppers getting there on their own. You'd have to take national brands off the shelf to make room, and when you do that here, people notice. They ask for the product at the service desk, and if we don't have it they go to the other grocer down the road. I'd rather not find out how many of them keep going there.

Interviewer: Have you seen that happen before?

Interviewee: We did a smaller version of it a while back in the snack aisle. They cut some brand facings to make room for a store-brand chip line. Sales in the aisle were flat to down, not up. Maybe the margin was better, I don't know, the finance people would know that. What I know is I had more complaints that quarter than I had all year.

Interviewer: What about the operational side? What does a change like this ask of your team?

Interviewee: That's the part nobody at headquarters thinks about. Every time we reset an aisle, it's labor. The last full reset we did on center store was something like 40 labor hours per aisle once you count pulling product, moving shelves, new tags, and fixing the planogram mistakes. And there are always planogram mistakes. If you're doubling private label, you're resetting most of the store. I'd need extra hours approved for that, and I don't usually get them.

Then there's supply. Our store-brand items run out more than the brands do. On store-brand milk we were seeing out-of-stocks around 6% of the time this summer. A national brand runs out, the vendor rep is in my store the next day. Store brand runs out, I put in a ticket and wait.

Interviewer: Is there anywhere you think more private label would work well in your store?

Interviewee: Yes, I don't want to sound completely negative. Organic and premium. If we had a good store-brand organic line, better looking, priced a little under the natural brands, I think my customers would try that. They're not looking for cheap, they're looking for good value for good stuff. The problem is our private label says "cheap" right now. If it said "smart" or "quality" that's a different conversation. Also household basics could go further. Paper towels, dish soap. Nobody's emotional about dish soap.

Interviewer: What would have to be true for you to support the goal?

Interviewee: Three things. One, the product has to be as good as the brand, not almost as good. Two, the packaging has to look like something you'd put on your counter. Three, I need labor and I need the supply chain to keep it in stock. If those three are in place, I'd support growing it. I just think doubling in two years is aggressive for a store like mine. Maybe that's possible in some of the other stores. You should talk to the managers in the value markets, they'll probably tell you something different.

Interviewer: Anything else you'd want leadership to hear?

Interviewee: Just that one number for the whole chain doesn't fit every store. Let the stores flex. If my store grows private label by a few points that's a big win for my customers, even if it's not doubling. Don't make us hit the same target as a store where half the customers are on a fixed budget. It's a different business.

Interviewer: That's really helpful. Thank you.

Interviewee: Of course. Happy to help.
