Interview ID: INT02
Role: Store manager (urban store, value-focused neighborhood)
SYNTHETIC: fictional interview for demonstration

Interviewer: Thanks for joining. To start, can you describe your store and who shops there?

Interviewee: Yeah. We're in the city, older store, smaller footprint than most. Lot of working families, a lot of seniors on fixed income, and a good number of folks paying with SNAP. People here watch every dollar. They read the unit price tags. If you move the price on eggs even a little, I hear about it by lunchtime.

Interviewer: How does private label perform for you today?

Interviewee: Really well. We're at about 27% of sales in private label, and I think we could be higher if we had more of it. The last couple of years, with prices going up on everything, I've watched people trade down in real time. Shopper comes in, picks up the brand-name pasta sauce, sees the price, puts it back and grabs ours. That happens all day.

Interviewer: So what's your reaction to the idea of doubling private label across the chain?

Interviewee: Honestly? It's overdue. I know some of the suburban stores will push back, and I get why, their customers are different. But in my store, I've got customers asking me for store-brand items we don't carry. Store-brand baby wipes, store-brand frozen vegetables in the bigger bags, store-brand coffee that isn't terrible. When I don't have it, they go to the discounter up the street. That store opened a few years ago and I felt it. I'm not going to beat them on everything, but private label is how I stay in the game.

Interviewer: You mentioned coffee "that isn't terrible." Tell me more about quality.

Interviewee: [laughs] Yeah, our coffee needs work. That's fair. Most of our line is fine. Canned goods, pasta, rice, dairy, bread, those are as good as the brand and people know it. But there's a few items where you can tell. Coffee, some of the frozen meals, the cookies. If we're going to double, those have to get better. Otherwise we're just putting more of something people don't want on the shelf.

Interviewer: How do you think about the margin side of it?

Interviewee: I'm not a finance guy, but I see the store P&L. Private label items run roughly 8 points more gross margin than the brand equivalent in my store, sometimes more. So even if I sell it cheaper to the customer, the store makes more on it. That's the whole point. I can give my customer a better price and the company still does better. That's a good trade.

Interviewer: Some people have raised concerns about store operations during a big transition. What's your view?

Interviewee: The resets are painful, no question. We're a small store, so there's less to reset, but my crew is also smaller. What I'd say is do it category by category, not all at once. Start with the places where customers are already asking. Don't try to do the whole store in one quarter. And give us some labor hours to do it right. A bad reset is worse than no reset.

Also, and this is a big one, keep it in stock. The worst thing that can happen is I train a customer to buy our brand of rice and then it's not there on Saturday. Then they go buy the national brand or they go to the discounter, and they don't come back to ours. We had a stretch last winter where store-brand canned beans were out for what felt like forever. That hurt.

Interviewer: What about competition? How do the other grocers factor in?

Interviewee: The discounters are eating everybody's lunch. Their whole store is basically private label. People used to think that was cheap stuff, now they think it's smart. My customers talk about it. "I got this at the discounter, it's just as good." We're kind of stuck in the middle if we don't move. We're not the fancy store and we're not the cheapest store. Private label is how we get a real price story.

Interviewer: If the chain goes ahead, what would have to be true for it to work?

Interviewee: The product has to be good. The price gap has to be real, like 20 to 30% under the brand, not five cents. You have to keep it in stock. And you have to let the stores that are ready go first. Let my store be the test. I'll show you what happens.

Interviewer: Is there anything that worries you about it?

Interviewee: That the company does it for the margin and forgets about the customer. If they just swap everything to store brand and don't lower the price, people will feel it. My customers are not dumb. They'll see the brand is gone and the store brand costs almost the same, and they'll feel like we pulled something. That's how you lose trust.

Interviewer: Great, thank you. This is really useful.

Interviewee: Anytime. And tell them to fix the coffee.
