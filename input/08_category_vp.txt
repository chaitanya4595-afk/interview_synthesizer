Interview ID: INT08
Role: Category VP (headquarters, private label and center store)
SYNTHETIC: fictional interview for demonstration

Interviewer: Thanks for your time. Can you start with where Northfield's private label program stands today?

Interviewee: Sure. We're at about 19% of sales in private label across the chain. That varies a lot by store. Some of our value-market stores are in the high twenties, some of the suburban stores are in the low teens. For context, the peer average for regional grocers our size is around 25%, and the discounters are much higher, most of their assortment is private label. So we're behind, and the gap has been widening in recent years.

Interviewer: Where did the idea of doubling come from?

Interviewee: It came from the board, honestly, after looking at what some other regional chains have done. The logic is straightforward. Our private label items carry a gross margin somewhere around 32 to 35%, compared to about 25% on the comparable national brands. Every point of share we shift is meaningful profit, and it gives us a price story against the discounters. Doubling is the ambition. I'll be honest, I think that's a stretch in two years.

Interviewer: What do you think is realistic?

Interviewee: I think a real step up in two years is achievable with real effort, and doubling is possible over a longer horizon. To double in two, we'd need roughly 400 new items, a full packaging redesign, significant supplier investment, and store resets across every store. My team is small. We'd have to at least double the team, and even then it's aggressive.

Interviewer: What are the biggest obstacles?

Interviewee: Quality perception is number one. We have a brand that a lot of customers see as the "cheap option," and some of that is deserved. We cut specs years ago to hit price points, and we have a handful of items, coffee is the one everyone mentions, that just aren't good enough. You can't double share with a brand people are embarrassed to put in their cart. So a big part of this is a brand relaunch, not just more items.

Number two is supply. In dry grocery, I'm fairly comfortable, there's capacity in the market. In dairy, frozen, and fresh it's much tighter. We'd probably need to bring on new suppliers in those categories, and qualifying a new supplier takes a long time.

Number three is stores. I know store managers are nervous about resets and about losing brand-loyal customers. They're not wrong. We tested an expanded private label set in snacks a couple of years ago and it didn't go well. Sales were flat and we got complaints. In hindsight we picked the wrong category. Snacks are brand-driven. We should have started with something like frozen vegetables or household paper, where people don't care as much.

Interviewer: Some store managers have said a single chain-wide target doesn't fit every store. How do you respond?

Interviewee: I agree with them, actually. I'd set the target at the chain level but let it flex by store cluster. The value-market stores can probably go faster and higher, the suburban stores slower. That's a more realistic plan and it's more likely to get buy-in. The board likes a single number, but I think we can show them a plan by cluster that adds up.

Interviewer: What about the national brand vendors? How do they factor in?

Interviewee: That's a real risk that doesn't get talked about enough. We get meaningful trade funding from national brands, promotional money, slotting fees, co-op advertising. If we cut their space significantly, some of that goes away. I'd estimate we could lose a meaningful part of trade funds in the categories we shift most aggressively. That has to be netted against the margin gain. Finance is working on that model now.

Interviewer: If you had to say what must be true for doubling to work, what would you say?

Interviewee: The product has to be as good as the brand in the categories we push. The packaging has to look modern. The price gap has to be big enough that customers feel it. Supply has to be reliable, meaning in stock at least as often as the brands. Stores need labor to execute the resets. And we need suppliers to commit capacity, which means we need to commit volume to them. If any one of those fails, the whole thing gets a lot harder.

Interviewer: Anything you'd want this work to answer?

Interviewee: I'd want to know which categories to go after first. Where customers are ready, where suppliers have capacity, where the margin is best. If we can find the handful of categories that tick all three boxes, we can build momentum and show the board it's working. Then we earn the right to go further.

Interviewer: Very helpful, thank you.

Interviewee: Thanks for doing this.
