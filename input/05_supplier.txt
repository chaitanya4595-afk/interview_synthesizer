Interview ID: INT05
Role: Private label supplier (dairy and frozen manufacturer)
SYNTHETIC: fictional interview for demonstration

Interviewer: Thank you for your time. Could you tell us about your company and what you make for Northfield?

Interviewee: We're a regional dairy and frozen manufacturer. For Northfield we make store-brand milk, yogurt, sour cream, cottage cheese, and some frozen items: ice cream, frozen vegetables, a couple of frozen breakfast products. We also make branded products under our own label, and we co-pack for a few other retailers.

Interviewer: How would you react if Northfield asked you to support doubling its private label volume over two years?

Interviewee: I'd tell them I'd love to, and then I'd tell them we can't do it without a lot of help. We're tight. Dairy and frozen aren't like dry goods where you can run another shift and store the extra in a warehouse. We're dealing with cold chain, short shelf life, and very expensive equipment. Our plant is running at close to 90% most of the year, and in summer we're basically at capacity because of ice cream.

Interviewer: How much more could you take on?

Interviewee: Without new capital, maybe 20% more volume across Northfield's lines, and that's pushing it. To go beyond that, we'd need a new filling line and more cold storage. Roughly $4 million in capital, and well over a year to get it built and running. That's not something we'd do on a handshake. We'd need a long-term contract that justifies the investment.

Interviewer: So you're more skeptical about the two-year timeline?

Interviewee: Yes. For dairy and frozen, doubling in two years is not realistic unless they bring in more suppliers, and bringing in new suppliers has its own risks. You're qualifying a new plant, running audits, testing product. That takes time too. And quality can slip. If a customer gets a bad carton of milk, they don't blame us, they blame the store brand. Then they don't buy the store brand anything.

Interviewer: Have you seen quality issues with Northfield's line?

Interviewee: We had one issue a couple of years ago with a yogurt. It was a stabilizer change the retailer asked for to hit a cost target. The texture changed, customers noticed, and the complaints came in. We changed it back. That's a good example of what I'd warn them about. When you push too hard on cost, quality suffers, and in dairy people notice immediately.

Interviewer: What do you think about the margin opportunity for Northfield?

Interviewee: It's real, but it's smaller in dairy than people think. Milk is basically a commodity. The store makes a thin margin on store-brand milk and an even thinner one on the brand. The bigger margin opportunity is in value-added stuff: Greek yogurt, specialty cheese, premium ice cream. That's where national brands charge a big premium and a store brand can undercut them and still make money. But those products are also harder to make well.

Interviewer: What about pricing pressure from your side? Input costs?

Interviewee: Milk prices swing a lot. We can't control that. Energy costs matter a lot for us because refrigeration and freezing use a lot of power. Our energy bill is up significantly. And labor, it's hard to hire people to work in a cold plant before dawn. We're paying more for that than we used to.

Interviewer: What would Northfield need to do to make this work with suppliers like you?

Interviewee: First, be realistic about categories. Don't treat dairy and frozen like dry grocery. Set different targets. Second, if they want us to invest, commit. A long-term agreement with volume floors, something like that. Third, don't change specs to shave pennies. Pick a quality level and stick with it. Fourth, pay faster. We're on 60-day terms right now, which is tough for a business like ours with high fixed costs. If they want us to grow with them, faster payment would help a lot.

Interviewer: Do you think the goal is achievable at all?

Interviewee: Across the whole store? Maybe. They'd have to get most of the growth from dry grocery, household, health and beauty, places where there's more capacity out there. In dairy and frozen I'd plan for a solid increase over two years, not doubling. If they need doubling in total, somebody else has to make up the difference.

Interviewer: Is there anything else they should know?

Interviewee: Just that the good private label programs I've seen take years to build. Retailers who try to do it fast usually end up with a lot of new items that don't sell and a lot of angry suppliers. The ones who do it well pick a few categories, get them really right, and build from there. I'd rather be part of that kind of program than a rushed one.

Interviewer: Thank you, this has been very helpful.

Interviewee: Happy to help.
